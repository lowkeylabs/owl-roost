# Interpreting the Evidence

The preceding tables summarize retirement outcomes produced under multiple investment return environments.

The objective of this study is **not** to identify the single "best" analytical run or to predict future investment returns.

Instead, the objective is to determine whether a proposed retirement planning decision remains appropriate across a broad range of plausible future market conditions.

The evidence should therefore be interpreted as a measure of **decision robustness** rather than as a forecast.

---

## Recommended Review Process

Evaluate the evidence in a consistent order.

1. Identify the planning decision suggested by the evidence.
2. Examine the uncertainty associated with that decision (for example, p10, median, and p90).
3. Determine whether the recommendation remains consistent across multiple analytical methods and market environments.
4. Evaluate decision margins and response surfaces.
5. Consider planning tradeoffs.
6. Assess the overall confidence supported by the evidence.

Recommendations should be based upon the complete body of evidence rather than any single analytical run or statistical summary.

---

## Decision Robustness

Retirement planning decisions should remain appropriate under many plausible future market environments.

A robust strategy produces similar planning recommendations despite changing assumptions.

A fragile strategy produces substantially different recommendations as market conditions change.

When reviewing the evidence:

* Look for recommendations that remain stable across multiple historical regimes and stochastic analyses.
* Identify recommendations that depend primarily upon unusually favorable assumptions.
* Describe whether the recommendation appears broadly applicable or highly conditional.

The objective is not to identify the highest-performing scenario.

The objective is to identify recommendations that remain appropriate despite uncertainty.

---

## Examine the Distribution

The median represents the typical outcome.

Planning decisions should also consider the range of plausible outcomes.

For every planning decision:

* Compare the median outcome.
* Compare the lower uncertainty bound (for example, p10).
* Compare the upper uncertainty bound (for example, p90).
* Determine whether the recommended planning decision changes across the uncertainty range.

Distinguish between:

* expected outcomes (median),
* downside robustness (lower-tail outcomes), and
* upside opportunity (upper-tail outcomes).

Recommendations should not rely solely on median outcomes unless the uncertainty analysis demonstrates that the recommendation is largely insensitive to plausible future conditions.

---

## Decision Margins

Decision margins describe **how close a recommendation is to changing**.

A recommendation may appear optimal simply because one alternative produces the highest reported value.

However, if several nearby alternatives produce materially similar outcomes, the precise mathematical optimum may be unimportant.

When reviewing the evidence, ask:

* Would a modest increase or decrease in the recommendation materially change retirement outcomes?
* Is the preferred recommendation clearly superior, or only marginally better?
* Would a simpler or more conservative recommendation perform nearly as well?
* Do several nearby alternatives form a broad, acceptable planning range?

Whenever practical, describe the range of recommendations supported by the evidence rather than emphasizing a single numerical optimum.

---

## Practical Significance

Not every numerical difference should influence a planning recommendation.

Distinguish between:

* differences that are numerically observable; and
* differences that are large enough to affect a retirement planning decision.

Small numerical improvements that would not materially change spending, taxes, financial security, or long-term outcomes should generally be treated as equivalent.

Preference may reasonably be given to recommendations that are simpler, easier to explain, more conservative, or easier to revisit during future planning cycles.

---

## Sensitivity

Sensitivity describes how strongly retirement outcomes respond to changing assumptions.

Questions to consider include:

* Do small changes in market assumptions produce only minor changes in retirement outcomes?
* Or do similar changes produce substantially different spending levels, taxes, or terminal wealth?
* Which planning decisions appear most sensitive to investment returns?

Strategies exhibiting low sensitivity generally require less accurate prediction of future market performance.

Highly sensitive strategies may require more frequent monitoring and periodic adjustment.

---

## Response Surfaces

Many retirement planning decisions can be viewed as response surfaces.

A response surface describes how retirement outcomes change as planning assumptions or decision variables change.

Flat response surfaces indicate that many nearby planning decisions produce materially similar outcomes.

These broad plateaus generally provide greater flexibility and confidence because the recommendation does not depend upon identifying a precise mathematical optimum.

Steep response surfaces indicate that relatively small changes in assumptions or planning decisions produce materially different outcomes.

These situations generally warrant additional analysis before making a recommendation.

---

## Tradeoffs

Retirement planning rarely optimizes a single objective.

Improving one objective often requires sacrificing another.

Examples include:

* increasing retirement spending may reduce terminal wealth;
* reducing taxes may require lower near-term spending;
* preserving greater financial flexibility may reduce expected lifetime consumption; and
* increasing Roth conversions may improve long-term tax efficiency while reducing near-term liquidity.

Recommendations should explicitly explain:

* which objectives are being prioritized;
* which objectives are being sacrificed; and
* why the proposed balance is appropriate.

---

## Confidence

Confidence reflects the consistency of the evidence supporting a recommendation.

Confidence generally increases when:

* multiple analytical methods support similar conclusions;
* recommendations remain stable across p10, median, and p90 outcomes;
* nearby planning decisions produce materially similar results; and
* recommendations remain appropriate across many market environments.

Confidence is lower when recommendations change substantially across analytical methods, uncertainty ranges, or nearby planning decisions.

Recommendations should communicate both the proposed planning decision and the degree of confidence supported by the evidence.

---

## Uncertainty

Future investment returns cannot be predicted with certainty.

The numerical values presented in this study should therefore not be interpreted as forecasts.

Instead, they describe how the proposed retirement strategy performs across a range of plausible market environments.

The objective is not to determine which future will occur.

The objective is to determine whether the proposed planning decision remains appropriate despite uncertainty.

---

## Forming Conclusions

Separate **observations** from **recommendations**.

Observations describe patterns present in the evidence.

Recommendations describe planning actions supported by those observations.

Conclusions should:

* distinguish observations from recommendations;
* identify recommendations that remain consistent across multiple analytical methods and market environments;
* discuss expected outcomes together with uncertainty (for example, p10, median, and p90);
* evaluate decision margins and practical significance;
* describe important tradeoffs among competing planning objectives;
* explain the confidence supported by the evidence;
* identify the principal sources of uncertainty; and
* acknowledge where additional evidence would materially improve confidence.

When multiple recommendations are supported by the evidence, acknowledge that more than one reasonable planning decision may exist.

Recommendations should emerge from consistent patterns observed across the complete body of evidence rather than isolated analytical runs or unusually favorable scenarios.
